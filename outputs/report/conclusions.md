

## Cleaning and Preprocessing

The raw dataset was already in good shape. It had no duplicate rows and no duplicate customer IDs. The only real problem was 11 blank TotalCharges values. All 11 belonged to customers with a tenure of 0 months, so we set them to $0. That is their true value, and it let us keep all 7,043 customers. We also changed "No internet service" and "No phone service" to "No". This avoided extra dummy columns, because InternetService and PhoneService already hold that information. We added three new features: NumServices, AvgMonthlySpend, and TenureGroup. We used one hot encoding with drop_first to avoid inventing a false order and to prevent multicollinearity. We split the data 80/20 with stratification before scaling, and we fit the scaler on the training set only. This kept test data out of training, so our scores reflect real performance. The final dataset had 28 features, with 5,634 training rows and 1,409 test rows.

## Exploratory Data Analysis

The EDA showed that churn depends mostly on how committed a customer is. Contract type had the biggest effect. Month to month customers churned at 42.7%, while customers on two year contracts churned at about 3%. Tenure was the strongest numeric predictor, with a correlation of −0.35. Customers who left had a median tenure of 10 months, compared with 38 months for those who stayed. Churn was 47% in the first year and fell to 10% after four years. MonthlyCharges had a weaker positive link with churn (0.19), so customers with higher bills left a bit more often. The IQR method found no outliers in tenure, MonthlyCharges, or TotalCharges, so we removed none. The overall churn rate of 26.5% confirmed that the classes are imbalanced, and this shaped how we trained and judged the models.

## Model Training

We trained Logistic Regression and Random Forest with class_weight="balanced" to deal with the 26.5% churn rate. We tuned both models with 5 fold GridSearchCV on the training set, scored by F1, because accuracy alone hides poor churn detection. A model that predicts "everyone stays" scores 73.5% accuracy but catches zero churners. The best settings were C = 0.01 for Logistic Regression, and max_depth = 10 with 300 trees for Random Forest. Tuning helped Random Forest the most. Limiting tree depth cut overfitting, which raised recall from 63.6% to 77.3% and F1 from 0.591 to 0.628. Logistic Regression barely changed after tuning (F1 went from 0.612 to 0.614), which shows the default model was already close to its best.

## Model Comparison Results

The tuned Random Forest performed best overall. It had the highest F1 (0.628) and tied for the highest ROC AUC (0.842). The tuned Logistic Regression came very close, with an F1 of 0.614 and an AUC of 0.839. Both tuned models caught 289 of the 374 churners in the test set, a recall of 77.3%. The difference was in false alarms. Random Forest wrongly flagged 258 loyal customers, and Logistic Regression flagged 278. This gave Random Forest slightly better precision (52.8% vs 51.0%) and accuracy (75.7% vs 74.2%). Both models agreed on the main drivers of churn: short tenure, month to month contracts, fiber optic internet, and electronic check payments. Logistic Regression adds direction to these findings. Two year contracts and longer tenure push customers toward staying, while electronic checks and fiber optic push them toward leaving.

## Overall Conclusion

This project shows that we can predict customer churn well with standard, interpretable models. The tuned Random Forest is our recommended model because it has the best F1 and AUC. The gap between the two models is small, though. If the business needs to explain to managers why a customer is at risk, **Logistic Regression** is a strong choice because its coefficients show which way each feature pushes. Both models find about three out of four customers who will leave. The cost is that roughly half of the flagged customers would have stayed anyway. The company can adjust the decision threshold depending on whether losing a customer costs more than sending an unneeded retention offer. The results point to clear actions. The company should focus retention efforts on customers in their first year, move month to month customers onto longer contracts, and look into why fiber optic and electronic check customers leave more often. Next steps could include testing other algorithms such as gradient boosting and adding cost data to choose the best threshold.
